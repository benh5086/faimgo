# Freelancing Your Skill — Your 30/60/90 Playbook

## Who this is for

You have a skill people already pay for — writing, design, coding, admin, marketing, editing, bookkeeping, whatever it is — and you want to turn it into income on the side, starting from wherever you are today. You don't need a website, a brand, a logo, or an audience. You need one clear offer, a short list of the right people, and the nerve to send ten messages. That's the whole first month.

**The honest promise:** if you follow this, your first paying client is realistic in 1–3 weeks. Not a fantasy income, not "passive" — real money from real work, fast, and then a system to make it repeatable.

## The one idea that makes this work

Most people fail at freelancing for one reason: they try to sell "I do design" (or writing, or coding) to "anyone." That's invisible. The people who land clients fast sell **one specific outcome to one specific kind of person**. "I help SaaS startups turn their messy help docs into clean, searchable guides" beats "freelance writer" every time — because the buyer instantly knows it's for them.

You are not competing with every freelancer on earth. You're competing to be the obvious choice for a narrow slice. Narrow wins.

## How to use this

Do it in order, one week at a time. Don't skip ahead to build a website — that's procrastination dressed up as work. The plan below assumes a few hours a week; if you have more, you'll move faster, and if you have less, stretch each week — the order doesn't change. Notes at the end show how it flexes for your hours, timeline, and experience level.

## Phase 1 — Days 1–30: Land your first paying client

### Week 1 — Define your one offer

The entire month hinges on this. Spend the time here.

1. **List the skill people already ask you for.** Not what you wish you did — what you're already the "go-to" for. If colleagues or friends ask you to "just take a look at" something, that's the signal.
2. **Pick one type of buyer.** Who has this problem *and* has money to fix it? Small business owners, startup founders, other freelancers who are drowning, local service businesses, real-estate agents, coaches. Pick one. You can change later.
3. **Write your offer in one sentence:** *"I help [type of person] get [specific outcome] by [what you do]."* Example: "I help real-estate agents get more listing inquiries by writing property descriptions that actually sell." Draft five versions; keep the one that makes a specific person nod.

**Done when:** you can say your offer out loud in one breath and it names a specific person and a specific outcome.

### Week 2 — Build the minimum to be hireable (and nothing more)

You need exactly three things, all free, none of them a website:

- **One place to show you're real.** A clean one-page Notion or Google Doc: your offer at the top, 2–3 examples of your work (or, if you have none yet, one sample you make this week for a made-up but realistic client), and a line on how to reach you. That's your "portfolio." A link is all you need.
- **A way to get paid.** Set up a free invoicing tool (Wave, or PayPal/Stripe payment links). Don't overthink it — you just need to be able to send a request for money.
- **Your rate.** Pick a number that feels slightly uncomfortable — that's usually about right. Start with a flat price per project, not per hour (hourly punishes you for being fast). If you're unsure, price your first project low *in exchange for a testimonial*, and raise it after.

**Done when:** you have a link you could paste to a stranger and a way to invoice them.

### Week 3 — Make your list and start pitching

This is where most people freeze. Push through it — it's just messages.

1. **Build a list of 20 real prospects** who fit your buyer. Start with people you have any connection to (past colleagues, your network, local businesses you use), then add cold ones you find on LinkedIn, Google Maps, or industry directories.
2. **Send 10 personalized first messages this week.** Not a mass blast — each one names something specific about *them*. Use the template below. Volume beats polish: 10 decent messages beat 2 perfect ones.
3. **Track it in a simple sheet:** name, how you know them, date messaged, reply, next step. This is your pipeline.

**Done when:** 10 real, personalized pitches are out the door.

### Week 4 — Follow up, close, deliver

- **Follow up once** with everyone who didn't reply (most replies come from the follow-up, not the first message).
- **When someone's interested,** don't over-negotiate. Confirm the outcome, the price, and the timeline in a short message, send the invoice (or a deposit), and start.
- **Deliver a little more than promised.** Your first client isn't just income — they're your first testimonial and your first referral source, which are worth more than the fee.

**Done when:** you've earned your first dollar — or, if not yet, you have live conversations in progress and a clear reason why (usually: pitch more, the numbers are just low).

> **Reality check for Week 4:** if you sent 10 pitches and got zero replies, the problem is almost always the *list* (wrong people) or the *offer* (too vague), not you. Don't quit — fix the offer to be more specific, or the list to be better-fit, and send 10 more. Landing the first client is a numbers game with a targeting problem hiding inside it.

## Phase 2 — Days 31–60: Turn one client into repeatable money

One project is a fluke. A repeatable system is a business. This month is about turning the thing that worked into something that keeps working.

- **Ask your first client two questions:** "Would you leave me a short testimonial?" and "Is there anyone else you know who needs this?" Both, every time. Referrals convert far better than cold outreach.
- **Turn the one-off into a retainer where it fits.** Instead of "I wrote your listing," offer "I'll handle four listings a month for $X." Recurring beats one-off — it's predictable income and less selling.
- **Raise your rate for the next client.** You have proof now. The second client should pay more than the first. Keep nudging until you hit mild resistance — that's your ceiling, and it's higher than you think.
- **Keep the pipeline warm.** Send 5 fresh pitches a week even while you're delivering. The reason freelancers ride a feast-famine rollercoaster is they stop pitching when they get busy. Don't.

**Done when:** you have at least one testimonial, one referral or repeat project, and a rate that's higher than where you started.

---

## Phase 3 — Days 61–90: Grow into steady income

Now you shape it into something that fits your life and compounds.

- **Productize your offer.** Turn "custom work" into a named package with a fixed scope and price ("The Listing Refresh — 5 property descriptions, 3-day turnaround, $X"). Packages are easier to sell, easier to deliver, and easier to raise prices on.
- **Pick one channel and go deeper.** Referrals, LinkedIn posts about your niche, or a specific platform — whichever brought your best client. Don't spread across five; double down on the one that worked.
- **Decide your ceiling on purpose.** Do you want this to stay a tidy side income (cap your clients, keep it simple) or grow toward replacing a salary (start raising rates hard, or subcontract the overflow)? Both are valid — choose it deliberately instead of drifting.
- **Protect your time.** By now demand can outrun your hours. Raise prices before you burn out; a higher rate with fewer clients is almost always better than the reverse.

**Done when:** you have steady, predictable income from this that you could keep running — and a clear decision about whether to keep it small or grow it.

## The tools (free first — don't spend until you've earned)

- **Portfolio:** Notion (free) or a Google Doc. No website needed for your first clients.
- **Invoicing / payments:** Wave (free), or PayPal / Stripe payment links.
- **Pipeline tracking:** a Google Sheet. Five columns: name, source, date pitched, reply, next step.
- **Writing / drafting help:** free AI assistants to speed up drafts — but your judgment is the product, so always edit.
- **Scheduling (once you have calls):** Calendly free tier.

Rule: don't pay for a single tool until it's saving you time you're already being paid for.

## The templates (copy, paste, personalize)

**The offer (your one sentence):**
> "I help [specific person] get [specific outcome] by [what you do]."

**The first outreach message (keep it short, make it about them):**
> Hi [Name] — I came across [specific thing about them: their site, their listing, their post] and noticed [specific, genuine observation]. I help [their type] with [outcome] — I put together [a quick idea / a sample / a thought] for you. Want me to send it over? No pressure either way.

**The follow-up (send once, a few days later):**
> Hi [Name] — just floating this back up in case it got buried. Happy to share that [idea/sample] whenever's useful. If now's not the time, no worries at all.

**Confirming the work (before you start):**
> Great — here's what I'll do: [outcome], delivered by [date], for [price]. I'll send an invoice to get us started. Anything you'd add before I dive in?

**Asking for the testimonial + referral (after you deliver):**
> Really glad that worked out. Two quick things: would you be open to a short line I could use as a testimonial? And — is there anyone else you know who could use the same thing? Happy to take great care of them.

## Common mistakes (the ones that actually sink people)

- **Building a website instead of pitching.** The website is procrastination. Clients come from messages, not from a homepage nobody visits.
- **Selling "I do X" instead of "I get Y for Z people."** Vague = invisible.
- **Charging by the hour.** It caps your income and punishes speed. Price the outcome.
- **Stopping outreach when you get busy.** This is what causes the famine half of feast-or-famine.
- **Waiting to feel "ready."** You get ready by pitching, not before.

## How this flexes for you

- **If you're experienced (already done this professionally):** skip lighter over Week 1–2; your offer and proof already exist. Go straight to the list and pitches — you can often land a client in the first week.
- **If you're brand new to freelancing but have the skill:** spend real time on Week 1's offer and make one strong sample in Week 2. Your first project can be priced low in exchange for a testimonial — that's not underselling, it's buying proof.
- **If you have under 5 hours a week:** the order is identical, just slower — treat each "week" as two. The one thing not to stretch: keep *some* pitches going out every week. Momentum is the whole game.
- **If you need money this week specifically:** freelancing usually pays in 1–3 weeks, not days — so if that's you, pair this with a faster first-dollar path (that's exactly what your Faimgo results already suggested), and let the freelancing income build underneath it.
